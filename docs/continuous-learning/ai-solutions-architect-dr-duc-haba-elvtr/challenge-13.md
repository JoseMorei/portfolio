1. What safeguards or guidelines should be implemented when developing a large language model (LLM) application for a healthcare company that utilizes private data from nurses and doctors?

When designing an LLM application for a healthcare organization, the most critical requirement is ensuring that the system protects sensitive medical information and complies with healthcare privacy regulations. In the United States, healthcare data is regulated primarily by the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA), which requires strict protections around Protected Health Information (PHI). Any AI solution that processes clinical notes, patient records, or even internal feedback from doctors and nurses must operate within a framework that treats the LLM as part of the healthcare organization’s regulated data environment.

A fundamental safeguard is ensuring secure data handling and encryption throughout the entire lifecycle of the data. Healthcare data should be encrypted both in transit and at rest using modern encryption standards such as TLS for network communication and AES-256 for storage. In addition, access to data should be tightly controlled through role-based access control (RBAC). This means that only specific authorized roles—such as physicians, data scientists with clearance, or compliance officers—can access sensitive datasets used by the LLM system. Logging and auditing are also essential because healthcare regulations require organizations to maintain detailed records of who accessed sensitive data and when.

Another important guideline is data minimization and de-identification. The architecture of the system should ensure that the LLM does not ingest raw identifiable patient information unless absolutely necessary. Many healthcare AI systems implement preprocessing pipelines that automatically remove or mask identifiable details such as patient names, addresses, and identification numbers before the data reaches the model. These processes typically rely on natural language processing techniques that detect sensitive entities in clinical text and redact them. Even when de-identified data is used, monitoring is still necessary because models can sometimes infer identifying details through context.

A secure deployment strategy is also critical. Healthcare companies frequently avoid sending sensitive data to public AI services because the data might be stored or used to train external models. Instead, organizations often deploy LLMs within private cloud environments or on-premises infrastructure configured to meet healthcare compliance standards. If third-party AI vendors are used, the healthcare company must sign a Business Associate Agreement (BAA) with the vendor, which legally requires the vendor to comply with HIPAA privacy and security rules.

Finally, governance and oversight are essential. Healthcare AI systems must include human supervision mechanisms because LLMs can produce incorrect or hallucinated responses. A “human-in-the-loop” process ensures that clinicians review AI outputs before they influence patient care decisions. Regular model monitoring, bias evaluation, and security testing should also be implemented to ensure the system remains safe, reliable, and compliant over time.

1.  If the legal team has concerns about using user feedback to refine training for your LLM, could using synthetic data—such as feedback text generated by generative AI—be a sufficient alternative?

Synthetic data can be a valuable tool when legal teams are concerned about privacy or regulatory risks associated with using real user feedback. Synthetic data refers to artificially generated datasets that mimic the patterns and statistical characteristics of real data without containing actual identifiable information. In the context of LLM training, generative AI can create simulated feedback from doctors, nurses, or patients that resembles real interactions while avoiding exposure of sensitive data.

In healthcare environments, synthetic data can help development teams build prototypes, test model behavior, and simulate workflows without directly accessing confidential information. For example, an AI system designed to summarize clinical notes could initially be trained using synthetic clinical feedback that mirrors how physicians might comment on a system’s output. Because the data does not correspond to real individuals, the legal risks associated with privacy violations are significantly reduced.

However, synthetic data is rarely a perfect substitute for real-world feedback. Generative models produce synthetic data based on patterns learned from existing datasets, which means the synthetic output may replicate surface-level patterns but miss subtle nuances found in real clinical environments. Doctors and nurses often communicate using specialized terminology, contextual assumptions, and workflow-specific language that synthetic generators may not fully capture. As a result, models trained exclusively on synthetic data may perform well in controlled environments but struggle when deployed in real clinical workflows.

Research has also shown that synthetic data can introduce biases or distort statistical relationships present in the original datasets. For example, if the generative model producing synthetic feedback does not fully understand the complexity of healthcare decision-making, the resulting dataset might oversimplify real-world interactions. This could lead to models that respond in unrealistic ways or fail to recognize edge cases that occur frequently in practice.

Because of these limitations, many organizations adopt a hybrid approach. Synthetic data can be used during early development stages to reduce privacy risk and accelerate experimentation. Later in the process, carefully anonymized real user feedback can be incorporated under strict governance policies, sometimes using techniques such as differential privacy or federated learning. These methods allow models to benefit from real-world data while still protecting the identities and information of healthcare professionals and patients.

Ultimately, synthetic data can be a helpful mitigation strategy when legal teams are concerned about privacy. However, it should usually be viewed as a complementary resource rather than a complete replacement for real feedback. Systems intended for clinical use generally require validation using real-world data to ensure they accurately reflect how healthcare professionals actually interact with AI systems.
